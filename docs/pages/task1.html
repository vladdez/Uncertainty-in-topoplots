Task 1

Explanation of the task 1

Task 1 examined whether adding uncertainty information made the underlying spatial pattern in an EEG topoplot more difficult to recognize. On each trial, participants saw a target ERP pattern and two alternative topoplots. They selected the alternative that most closely matched the target. One alternative represented the same ERP-pattern class as the target, while the other represented a different class.

The task included three characteristic ERP patterns: MMN, P3, and N170. Each was shown using each of the seven visualization types. This produced 21 trials per participant, each on separate page. Trial order was randomized separately for every participant.

Trial reconstruction and presentation order

  • Trial information was reconstructed from the survey records and joined with each participant’s responses using participant, trial, and question identifiers.

Accuracy analysis

  • Every response was coded as correct (1) or incorrect (0).

  • The analysis contains 2,394 responses: 114 participants × 21 trials.

  • Visualization type was treated as a categorical variable, with the basic topoplot as the reference condition.

  • Presentation position was represented by the centered variable position_c, calculated as position - mean(position). Because positions ranged from 1 to 21, their mean was 11. Thus, position_c = 0 represents position 11, negative values represent earlier trials, and positive values represent later trials. This variable tests whether accuracy changed linearly as participants progressed through the task.

  • No responses were excluded for missing values. All 2,394 reconstructed records contained accuracy, visualization type, ERP pattern, presentation position, target stimulus, and distractor stimulus.

  • Stimulus assignment was incomplete but overlapping across participants. Across their 21 trials, participants saw 12–20 unique target stimuli (median 16), 13–20 unique distractors (median 15), and 18–28 unique stimulus identities across both roles (median 23). No participant saw all 30 stimulus identities. Because specific targets and distractors may differ in difficulty, this design motivates separate target- and distractor-stimulus random intercepts in the inferential model.

Observed accuracy

  • Accuracy was calculated as correct responses divided by all responses (correct / (correct + incorrect)). Each visualization type had 342 responses (114 participants × 3 trials). This is an unadjusted pooled proportion.

  • For the descriptive uncertainty display, each participant contributed one mean accuracy per visualization type, calculated from their three trials. Because every participant contributed the same number of trials, the across-participant mean equals the pooled proportion above.

  • The dark-blue region shows the two-sided 95% t-confidence interval around the across-participant mean; opacity decreases beyond that interval.

Population-average accuracy

  • A logistic mixed-effects model estimated the probability of a correct response:

    correct ~ vistype + pattern + position_c +
  (1 | ID) + (1 | stimulus) + (1 | wrong_image)

  • Some participants were generally more accurate than others. Because every participant completed 21 trials, we added a participant random intercept, which allows each participant to have their own baseline accuracy. The model then examines whether accuracy changed with visualization type or trial position across participants.

  • ERP pattern was included as a fixed effect, so visualization comparisons account for systematic accuracy differences among MMN, N170, and P3 trials.

  • Target and distractor stimuli were included as separate random intercepts. This gives each image its own baseline difficulty in each role and prevents unusually easy or difficult images from being mistaken for visualization effects.

  • These probabilities estimate average accuracy for each visualization type across the modeled participant and stimulus populations. They were calculated at the average trial position, averaged equally across the three ERP patterns, and integrated over modeled differences among participants, target stimuli, and distractor stimuli.

NoteChoosing how to summarize predicted accuracy

There are three ways to summarize the model:

Option Calculation What it describes
1 Set participant and stimulus differences to zero. Average-baseline participants and stimuli.
2 Average over the fitted participants, targets, and distractors. The levels observed in this study.
3 Average across modeled participant and stimulus differences. The modeled participant and stimulus populations.

We chose option 3 because our research question concerns average performance across participant and stimulus populations, not only average-baseline or observed levels (Hu et al., 1998; Hedeker et al., 2018).

Estimand sensitivity check. Across visualization types, the largest difference between the selected population-average probability and the zero-random-effect probability was 7.1 percentage points; the largest difference from the fitted participant-and-stimulus average was 0.4 percentage points. The reporting choice therefore does not materially change the pattern of results.

This does not make the sample representative of all EEG researchers.

NoteWhy participant-specific random slopes were not retained

We tested whether the effect of trial position differed between participants by adding a participant-specific position slope: (1 + position_c | ID), while retaining the ERP pattern and stimulus terms. The model produced a singular fit and could not estimate this additional variation:

  • The estimated variance of the position slopes was 0.00018.

  • The estimated correlation between the baseline accuracy and position slopes was -1.00.

Together, these boundary estimates indicate that the data did not contain enough information to estimate participant baseline differences and participant-specific position effects separately.

We also considered participant-specific effects for visualization type. However, each participant provided only three responses for each visualization. Modeling the seven-level visualization factor would require six additional slopes and their covariances, which the available responses could not support reliably.

We therefore retained the simpler random-intercept model. This does not show that the position and visualization effects are identical for every participant. It means only that these data cannot reliably estimate how those effects differ between participants.

Model details:
  • Model: Binomial mixed-effects model with a logit link: correct ~ vistype + pattern + position_c + (1 | ID) + (1 | stimulus) + (1 | wrong_image).
  • Reference visualization: Basic topoplot.
  • ERP pattern: Predictions were averaged equally across MMN, N170, and P3.
  • Trial position: Predicted accuracy was calculated at the average presentation position.
  • Random effects: Predictions were averaged across the modeled distributions of participant baseline accuracy and target- and distractor-stimulus difficulty.
  • Confidence intervals: A separate 95% confidence interval was calculated for each visualization type from 10,000 simulated sets of fixed-effect estimates generated with MASS::mvrnorm().
  • Limitation: The estimated participant and stimulus random-intercept variances were treated as fixed when calculating these intervals.

Conditional logistic paired-choice sensitivity analysis

Task 1 is a paired-choice experiment: every trial presents one matching and one nonmatching candidate image. We therefore fitted a conditional logistic paired-choice model as a sensitivity analysis. Its candidate-image terms provide a Bradley–Terry representation of each image’s general tendency to be selected. Unlike the primary accuracy model, it assigns each of the 30 candidate images one shared attractiveness parameter regardless of whether that image is matching or nonmatching on a particular trial. It also adjusts for whether an image appeared as the second option.

The data were represented by two rows per trial, with one row for each candidate and one selected candidate within each participant–trial choice set. Visualization effects were estimated as interactions with matching-candidate status because visualization type is constant within a choice set and its main effect therefore cancels. Standard errors were clustered by participant to account for each participant’s 21 choices.

NoteTerms explanation
  • Conditional: The model compares the two candidate images within the same participant’s trial. It uses the fact that exactly one candidate was selected and removes the separate baseline for each trial. The term does not refer to an experimental condition.
  • Stratum: A stratum is one participant–trial choice set. It contains two rows: one for the matching candidate and one for the nonmatching candidate. The model compares candidates within this set, not candidates from different trials.

Sensitivity-analysis conclusion

After accounting for candidate-image attractiveness, option position, ERP pattern, and presentation position, the joint participant-clustered Wald test indicated that visualization type changed preference for the matching candidate, \(\chi^2(6) = 12.81\), p = 0.0461. Relative to Basic topoplot, the adjusted odds of selecting the matching candidate were lower for Bivariate corners (OR = 0.59, 95% CI 0.39–0.90, p = 0.0139) and Bivariate range (OR = 0.61, 95% CI 0.41–0.91, p = 0.0158). These individual intervals are unadjusted for the six comparisons. The sensitivity analysis therefore supports the primary model’s pattern while separating matching performance from images’ general tendency to be selected; it does not replace the primary mixed-effects accuracy analysis.

  • Model: Binomial mixed-effects model with a logit link: correct ~ log_time_within + log_time_between_c + vistype + pattern + position_c + (1 | ID) + (1 | stimulus) + (1 | wrong_image).

At the participant level, accuracy and typical completion time had a Pearson correlation of 0.121 (95% CI -0.064 to 0.298, p = 0.2). In the adjusted mixed model, doubling the time spent on a trial was associated with an odds ratio of 1.079 for a correct response (95% CI 0.957 to 1.216). The between-participant odds ratio was 1.275 (95% CI 1.049 to 1.550).

All three estimates were small, and their intervals included the corresponding null value (zero for the correlation and one for the odds ratios). These data therefore provide little evidence that longer completion time was meaningfully associated with greater Task 1 accuracy.
