## Actionable principles

<a id="principle-05-choose-by-message"></a>

### Choose the chart from the message, not a chart-name habit

**Problem addressed:** A familiar chart can be a poor match for the comparison the reader must make.

**Recommended solution:** First classify the intended message—amount, distribution, proportion, relationship, geospatial pattern, or uncertainty—then choose among forms suited to that task.

**Why it works:** Wilke organizes the directory by what a figure communicates because task and comparison determine which encodings are useful.

**Applies when:** Early structural review before cosmetic refinement.

**Exceptions and qualifications:** The directory gives viable families, not a universal winner; later chapters supply task-specific tradeoffs.

**Book example:** Amounts may use bars or endpoint dots, while distributions may use histograms, densities, ECDFs, boxplots, violins, or ridgelines depending on the comparison; Figures 6-11 and 6-12 illustrate the task-dependent dot-versus-bar choice.

**Search terms:** `chart selection`, `message`, `task`, `structural change`

<a id="principle-05-proportion-form-follows-comparison"></a>

### Select a proportion chart by the required comparison

**Problem addressed:** One proportion display cannot simultaneously optimize whole–part perception, exact part comparison, and change across many conditions.

**Recommended solution:** Use pies for simple fractions/whole emphasis, side-by-side bars for comparing pieces, stacked bars for many condition sets, and stacked densities along a continuous variable.

**Why it works:** Each form provides a different baseline and allocates space differently, changing which comparison is easiest.

**Applies when:** Composition and proportion figures.

**Exceptions and qualifications:** These are conditional strengths; Wilke explicitly rejects a categorical ban on pie charts.

**Book example:** The directory contrasts pies, grouped bars, stacked bars, and stacked densities; Figures 10-1 and 10-3 show how the same parliamentary composition supports different comparisons.

**Search terms:** `pie chart`, `stacked bar`, `side-by-side bar`, `proportions`
