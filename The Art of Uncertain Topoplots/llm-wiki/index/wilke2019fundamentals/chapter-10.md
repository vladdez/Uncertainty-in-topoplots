## Actionable principles

<a id="principle-10-no-universal-proportion-chart"></a>

### Choose proportion charts by the comparison, not a blanket rule

**Problem addressed:** Categorically banning or favoring pie charts ignores what different forms make easy to see.

**Recommended solution:** Use pies for simple fractions and a small whole, side-by-side bars for direct part comparison, and stacked forms for many sets or time series.

**Why it works:** Pies emphasize whole and familiar fractions; aligned bars improve comparison; stacking is compact across conditions but gives interior parts shifting baselines.

**Applies when:** Part-to-whole figures.

**Exceptions and qualifications:** No form is consistently superior; dataset size and intended story decide.

**Book example:** Figures 10-1 to 10-3 show that a pie most clearly communicates the coalition's slight majority in the German Bundestag example.

**Search terms:** `pie chart`, `proportion`, `task`, `aligned baseline`

**Evidence:** passages [p113-b002](../../raw/wilke2019fundamentals.md#p113-b002); figures [Figure 10-1](../../raw/wilke2019fundamentals.md#p111-b001), [Figure 10-3](../../raw/wilke2019fundamentals.md#p113-b001).

<a id="principle-10-limit-stacked-comparison"></a>

### Use stacked sequences only when shifting baselines do not defeat the task

**Problem addressed:** Interior segments are hard to compare across stacks because both their starts and ends move.

**Recommended solution:** Prefer side-by-side bars for component comparison; stacking can remain clear with two parts or when the total/overall composition is primary.

**Why it works:** Aligned baselines support accurate comparison, whereas middle segments lack a common reference.

**Applies when:** Repeated compositions and time series.

**Exceptions and qualifications:** Two-part stacks avoid the interior-segment problem; reference lines can support threshold judgments.

**Book example:** Figure 10-5 obscures changes in middle companies; Figure 10-7 clearly shows a two-part gender composition and a 50% threshold.

**Search terms:** `stacked bars`, `shifting baseline`, `two categories`, `threshold`

**Evidence:** passages [p115-b001](../../raw/wilke2019fundamentals.md#p115-b001), [p116-b002](../../raw/wilke2019fundamentals.md#p116-b002); figures [Figure 10-5](../../raw/wilke2019fundamentals.md#p115-b002), [Figure 10-7](../../raw/wilke2019fundamentals.md#p117-b001).

<a id="principle-10-preserve-total-size-context"></a>

### Do not let normalized proportions hide changing totals

**Problem addressed:** A 100% composition can look stable even when the number of observations changes sharply.

**Recommended solution:** Show each component against the overall distribution or otherwise include absolute-size context; use relative panels only when conditional proportions are the question.

**Why it works:** The total distribution reveals whether a constant percentage represents many or few cases.

**Applies when:** Proportions across age, time, or another variable with nonuniform sample size.

**Exceptions and qualifications:** Relative panels are still useful for questions such as when a category exceeds 50%; absolute and relative views answer different questions.

**Book example:** Figures 10-8 to 10-12 contrast normalized health/marital composition with separate component-versus-total displays.

**Search terms:** `normalized proportion`, `denominator`, `absolute count`, `part versus total`

**Evidence:** passages [p118-b002](../../raw/wilke2019fundamentals.md#p118-b002), [p118-b003](../../raw/wilke2019fundamentals.md#p118-b003), [p120-b003](../../raw/wilke2019fundamentals.md#p120-b003); figures [Figure 10-9](../../raw/wilke2019fundamentals.md#p119-b001), [Figure 10-12](../../raw/wilke2019fundamentals.md#p121-b001).
