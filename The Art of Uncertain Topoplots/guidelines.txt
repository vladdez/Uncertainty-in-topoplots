# Writing Guidelines

## 1. Core identity of the paper

Do **not** frame this as merely an EEG application paper or as a competition asking which uncertainty topoplot is best.

The paper should use EEG/ERP topoplots as a concrete case through which we investigate broader visualization questions:

* How can uncertainty be added to an established spatial visualization without obscuring the primary signal?
* What are the trade-offs between richer uncertainty information, perceptual complexity, and interpretability?
* Do objective performance, subjective preference, and participants’ reasoning lead to the same conclusions?
* How should visualization designers account for established domain conventions and familiar encodings?
* What can domain-specific evaluation teach us about uncertainty visualization more generally?

EEG is the **case and testbed**; uncertainty visualization is the broader contribution.

The paper should also provide **practical recommendations for EEG/ERP researchers** on choosing and using uncertainty visualizations for topoplots. Translate the findings into guidance tied to analytical tasks, explaining which encodings may be useful, what trade-offs to expect, and what interpretation difficulties to watch for. Recommendations should be supported by the study's evidence and acknowledge its limitations. This practical guidance should complement the broader visualization contribution.

Avoid letting the manuscript become so EEG-specific that a visualization researcher outside neuroscience cannot see why the findings matter.

---

## 2. Main narrative

The paper should follow this logical chain:

**Domain problem → formative interviews → visualization requirements/design space → visualization designs → controlled evaluation → explanations of participant behaviour → broader visualization implications.**

The formative interviews are important. They are not supplementary anecdotes. They establish that the problem exists in real practice, identify how researchers currently reason about topoplots and uncertainty, and help motivate the later tasks and designs.

The quantitative study then asks what happens when those designs are actually used.

---

## 3. Task performance is not enough

Do not reduce the Results or Discussion to accuracy scores and statistical comparisons.

A central principle of the paper is:

**We need to know not only what participants chose, but also why they made those choices.**

Triangulate four kinds of evidence:

1. **Formative interviews** — what domain experts need and how they currently work.
2. **Objective performance** — whether participants can perform the tasks accurately.
3. **Subjective evaluation** — what participants prefer, dislike, or perceive as useful.
4. **Open-ended qualitative feedback** — participant-reported reasons for those judgments and decisions.

Use qualitative findings to help explain quantitative patterns. For example:

* Why might a visualization preserve accuracy but be disliked?
* Why might a visually appealing encoding cause interpretation problems?
* Which visual features caused confusion?
* Did participants ignore uncertainty even when it was available?
* Did some techniques require repeated legend lookup?
* Did familiarity with conventional topoplots influence judgments?

Do not infer psychological mechanisms that participants did not report. Distinguish clearly between **observed behaviour**, **participant-reported explanation**, and **our interpretation**.

---

## 4. Contribution framing

Every contribution must matter to visualization research, not only EEG.

Prefer contribution claims such as:

* empirical evidence about trade-offs in integrating uncertainty into an established spatial representation;
* a design space or systematic comparison of uncertainty encodings applied to the same underlying spatial signal;
* evidence showing where objective performance and subjective experience agree or diverge;
* qualitative evidence explaining why particular uncertainty encodings succeed or fail;
* design implications for uncertainty visualization and scientific visualization software.

Avoid weak contribution claims such as:

* “we implemented seven topoplots”;
* “we conducted a survey”;
* “we collected N participants”;
* “we applied existing methods to EEG.”

Those are methods, not contributions.

---

## 5. Guard against the narrow-application problem

Daniel's concern should be treated as a major writing constraint:

**A narrow EEG application can be a publication risk if the manuscript never gets back to visualization.**

Therefore:

### Introduction

Start from the broader visualization problem and introduce EEG topoplots as an important instance of it.

### Related Work

Connect to:

* uncertainty visualization;
* bivariate visualization;
* visual encoding of uncertainty;
* evaluation methodology;
* scientific visualization conventions/defaults;
* domain visualization only where necessary.

### Design section

Explain each technique in terms of its **visual encoding principle**, not merely its EEG implementation.

### Results

Report the experiment faithfully without forcing generalization.

### Discussion

Abstract from the EEG findings to broader visualization principles.

### Conclusion

End with what visualization researchers and visualization-tool designers can learn, not simply which EEG plot scored highest.

---

## 6. Use the EEG case to discuss larger visualization issues

The Discussion should explicitly consider themes such as:

### Signal vs. uncertainty competition

Adding uncertainty creates another visual variable that can compete with perception of the primary data.

### Information richness vs. readability

Encoding more information is not automatically better if users cannot efficiently decode it.

### Integral vs. separable representations

Adjacent plots, bivariate colour, markers, and animation impose different requirements for integrating the signal and uncertainty.

### Familiarity and conventions

A familiar representation may remain effective even when it carries less information. New uncertainty encodings must compete not only perceptually but also with learned conventions.

### Objective effectiveness vs. subjective acceptance

A visualization can perform well objectively yet receive poor subjective evaluations, or vice versa. Neither measure alone defines a successful visualization.

### Defaults shape scientific practice

Visualization libraries provide defaults that can become disciplinary conventions. Better uncertainty representations are useful only if they are interpretable and practical enough to be adopted.

### Evaluation methodology

Controlled performance studies reveal *whether* a representation works under a particular task; qualitative evidence helps explain *why* and whether the result is likely to matter in practice.

Do not claim that findings automatically generalize beyond EEG. Phrase broader conclusions as **design implications**, **lessons**, or **hypotheses for other spatial scientific visualizations** where appropriate.

---

## 7. Formative study → design

Make the causal role of the nine interviews explicit.

They should establish:

* current practices;
* how researchers understand uncertainty;
* where uncertainty is currently missing or difficult to assess;
* barriers to showing uncertainty;
* relevant analytical tasks;
* requirements that informed the quantitative study.

Connect these findings directly to later design decisions.

Do not present the interviews and quantitative experiment as two unrelated studies.

The narrative should make clear:

**what we learned → what we designed because of it → what we evaluated.**

---

## 8. Design rationale

For every visualization condition explain:

1. **What is encoded?**
2. **Which visual channel encodes voltage?**
3. **Which visual channel encodes uncertainty?**
4. **Why was this encoding chosen?**
5. **Which previous visualization work motivates it?**
6. **What trade-off is it expected to introduce?**

Do not describe a visualization only procedurally.

For adaptations of existing techniques, clearly distinguish:

* what comes from previous literature;
* what we modified;
* why we modified it.

---

## 9. Results writing

Results should answer the research questions rather than follow the order in which analyses happened.

Report:

* estimates;
* uncertainty/confidence intervals;
* effect sizes where appropriate;
* statistical evidence;
* relevant descriptive differences.

Do not turn every numerical difference into a finding.

Use **“statistically significant”** only for statistical significance, never as a synonym for important or large.

If evidence does not establish a difference from the basic topoplot, say so clearly.

Do not write:

> Method A was better.

when the evidence only supports:

> Method A had a higher estimated accuracy, but the interval/evidence did not establish a clear difference from the baseline.

Null and uncertain findings are part of the contribution.

---

## 10. Qualitative evidence

Do not use quotations merely as decoration.

Use qualitative analysis to identify recurring explanations or mechanisms, for example:

* difficulty mapping colours to a bivariate legend;
* visual clutter;
* uncertainty being easy to overlook;
* preservation or loss of the underlying signal pattern;
* cognitive effort;
* familiarity;
* perceived informativeness;
* preference for separating signal and uncertainty.

Report themes systematically and use representative quotations only where they add information.

Keep participant-level variation visible when it matters.

---

## 11. Discussion writing

The Discussion should not repeat Results.

For each major finding use approximately this logic:

**Finding → explanation from qualitative evidence → relationship to previous visualization literature → broader implication → limitation/caveat.**

Ask repeatedly:

> Why should a TVCG reader who never works with EEG care about this result?

If there is no convincing answer, either strengthen the connection or reduce the prominence of the finding.

---

## 12. Claims and citations

Every citation must have a job.

Use citations to support:

* established facts;
* previous methods;
* design rationale;
* theoretical arguments;
* claims about scientific practice;
* comparisons with previous findings.

Do not cite a paper merely because it is related.

When describing **our implementation, procedure, stimuli, or results**, external citations are normally unnecessary unless the method is adapted from prior work.

Prefer original sources over secondary citations.

Do not invent references or bibliographic information.

---

## 13. Writing style

Write concise scientific prose.

Wrap prose in `journal.typ` at approximately **90 characters per source line** to keep
the manuscript easy to read and edit. Use single line breaks within paragraphs and
preserve blank lines between paragraphs. Source wrapping must not change the wording,
paragraph structure, or rendered PDF layout. Keep Typst syntax, citation keys, URLs,
and code intact rather than splitting them to meet the line-length target.

Prefer:

> The bivariate encoding reduced chroma as SE increased.

over:

> It can clearly be seen that the proposed bivariate visualization technique makes use of a reduction in chroma in order to represent increasing SE.

Use:

* short sentences where possible;
* concrete nouns and verbs;
* consistent terminology;
* explicit logical transitions.

Avoid:

* “obviously,” “clearly,” and “of course”;
* unnecessary adjectives such as “novel,” “powerful,” or “excellent”;
* promotional language;
* vague statements such as “the results are interesting”;
* repeating the same result in Results, Discussion, and Conclusion;
* excessive EEG background that a visualization reader does not need.

Each paragraph should normally have **one main claim**.

---

## 14. Scientific caution

Keep separate:

* statistical evidence;
* practical importance;
* subjective preference;
* qualitative interpretation.

Do not imply causality from observational qualitative data.

Do not generalize from controlled experimental tasks to complete real-world EEG workflows.

Acknowledge ecological-validity limitations.

Do not claim that one visualization is universally “best.” The likely contribution is understanding **trade-offs and conditions under which different encodings work**, not identifying a universal winner.

---

## 15. IEEE/TVCG constraints

Keep the regular paper concise and aim for **≤12 formatted pages including references**. Move supporting but nonessential analyses to supplementary material.

The abstract must be a single, self-contained paragraph and accurately reflect the final results and implications.

The methodology must contain enough information for reproducibility.

Human-participant reporting must include the appropriate ethics/oversight statement and informed-consent information.

Where possible, make data, stimuli, analysis code, and visualization code available.

References should directly support the claims for which they are cited.

---

## 16. Final test for every section

Before considering a section finished, ask:

**Introduction:** Is the visualization research problem clear before the EEG details?

**Formative Study:** Can the reader see how these interviews changed or motivated the study?

**Design Space:** Does each design represent a meaningful visualization choice rather than just another implementation?

**Quantitative Study:** Are the research questions, tasks, and analyses reproducible?

**Results:** Are the claims proportional to the statistical evidence?

**Qualitative Results:** Do they explain participant reasoning rather than merely provide quotes?

**Discussion:** Does it connect the EEG findings back to visualization research?

**Conclusion:** Would a visualization researcher who never analyzes EEG understand what was learned?

The manuscript succeeds only if the answer to the last question is **yes**.
